Lesson · Average Acceleration

Topic 5 · how quickly velocity changes

When you sit in a vehicle that suddenly starts moving, you feel a jolt; when it stops suddenly, you feel a jolt again. Both are the feelings of a change in velocity. To measure how quickly the velocity changes, we use a new quantity.

Average acceleration

Average acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time interval. It is the rate of change of velocity with respect to time.

Since velocity is a vector, acceleration is also a vector: we must state its magnitude and its direction. Its SI unit is m s⁻² (metre per second squared), read as “metre per second per second”.

Average accelerationa = (v − u) ÷ tu = initial velocity, v = final velocity, t = time interval. If velocity changes from u at time t₁ to v at time t₂, then a = (v − u) ÷ (t₂ − t₁).

What does the sign of acceleration mean?

For motion in a straight line, if the magnitude of velocity is increasing, the average acceleration is in the direction of velocity (positive if velocity is positive).

If the magnitude of velocity is decreasing, the average acceleration is opposite to the direction of velocity — this is often called negative acceleration, retardation or deceleration. When you brake a bicycle, the acceleration is opposite to the direction of motion.

Note: An object can move very fast and still have zero acceleration. Acceleration depends not on how fast an object is moving, but on how quickly its velocity is changing. A bus on a straight highway at a steady 60 km h⁻¹ has zero acceleration.

Example 4.3: a bus on a highway

A bus moves at 36 km h⁻¹. The driver presses the accelerator for 10 s and the velocity becomes 54 km h⁻¹. Later the driver brakes and the bus stops in 5 s. Find the average acceleration in (i) the accelerating interval and (ii) the braking interval.

Solution. First convert the speeds: 36 km h⁻¹ = 36 × 1000 ÷ 3600 = 10 m s⁻¹ and 54 km h⁻¹ = 15 m s⁻¹.

(i) u = 10 m s⁻¹, v = 15 m s⁻¹, t = 10 s → a = (15 − 10) ÷ 10 = +0.5 m s⁻². The velocity is increasing, so the acceleration is in the direction of velocity.

(ii) u = 15 m s⁻¹, v = 0 m s⁻¹, t = 5 s → a = (0 − 15) ÷ 5 = −3 m s⁻². The minus sign shows that the acceleration is opposite to the direction of velocity, because the bus is slowing down.

Example 4.4: a falling object and the value of g

An object is dropped and its velocity is measured every second: 0, 9.8, 19.6, 29.4, 39.2 m s⁻¹. Find the average acceleration in each one-second interval.

Solution. From 0 s to 1 s: (9.8 − 0) ÷ 1 = 9.8 m s⁻². From 1 s to 2 s: (19.6 − 9.8) ÷ 1 = 9.8 m s⁻². From 2 s to 3 s: (29.4 − 19.6) ÷ 1 = 9.8 m s⁻². From 3 s to 4 s: (39.2 − 29.4) ÷ 1 = 9.8 m s⁻².

The acceleration is the same in every interval — it is constant at 9.8 m s⁻², which means the velocity increases by 9.8 m s⁻¹ every second. This special acceleration is the acceleration due to the gravitational force of the Earth, written g, and it acts in the direction of motion (downwards) here.

Ask and Understand

Acceleration, its sign and the special value g.

Q1Is a fast-moving object always accelerating?

No. Acceleration is about the change of velocity, not about how fast the object is moving. A jet flying at a steady 900 km h⁻¹ in a straight line has zero acceleration, while a bicycle just starting from rest accelerates as soon as it begins to move. What matters is whether the velocity is changing — in magnitude, in direction, or in both.

Q2What is negative acceleration? Is it the same as slowing down?

Negative acceleration means the acceleration points in the direction opposite to the chosen positive direction. If the object is moving in the positive direction, negative acceleration means it is slowing down (retardation). But if the object is moving in the negative direction, a negative acceleration makes it speed up in that direction. So the sign must always be read together with the direction of motion — the safest rule is: acceleration in the direction of velocity → speeding up; acceleration opposite to velocity → slowing down.

Q3Why does a falling body have a constant acceleration of 9.8 m s⁻²?

Because the Earth pulls every object towards itself with a force that stays practically the same near the surface, so the velocity of a freely falling body increases by equal amounts in equal intervals of time: 9.8, 19.6, 29.4, 39.2 m s⁻¹ after 1, 2, 3, 4 s. That is exactly the meaning of constant acceleration, and the value is called the acceleration due to gravity, g = 9.8 m s⁻². (The symbol g is used for its magnitude; its direction is towards the centre of the Earth.)

Q4Can acceleration be produced without any change of speed?

Yes — if the direction of the velocity changes while its magnitude stays the same. This happens in uniform circular motion, where an object moves round a circle at constant speed. Because velocity is a vector, a change in direction is also a change in velocity, so the motion is accelerated. In everyday language we usually notice acceleration only when a vehicle speeds up or slows down, but turning a corner at a steady speed also involves acceleration. Topic 10 studies exactly this case.

Book Questions — Explained

1. Activity 4.2: Using the 0 to 100 km h⁻¹ times of different cars, calculate the magnitude of average acceleration for each. Activity 4.2

Convert first: 100 km h⁻¹ = 27.8 m s⁻¹ and u = 0. For a car taking 5 s, a = 27.8 ÷ 5 = 5.6 m s⁻²; for 10 s, a = 2.8 m s⁻²; for 20 s, a = 1.4 m s⁻². The shorter the time taken to reach 100 km h⁻¹, the larger the average acceleration. Compare your results with the value of g = 9.8 m s⁻² — even a sports car accelerates at only about half of g.

2. Example 4.4: A dropped object has velocities 0, 9.8, 19.6, 29.4, 39.2 m s⁻¹ after 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 s. Find the average acceleration in every successive one-second interval. Is it constant? What is its direction? Example 4.4

Each interval gives (9.8 − 0) ÷ 1 = 9.8 m s⁻², (19.6 − 9.8) ÷ 1 = 9.8 m s⁻², (29.4 − 19.6) ÷ 1 = 9.8 m s⁻² and (39.2 − 29.4) ÷ 1 = 9.8 m s⁻². So the average acceleration is constant at 9.8 m s⁻². Because the velocity is increasing in the direction of motion, the acceleration is also in the direction of motion (downwards). This is the acceleration due to gravity, g.

3. Why is it important to fix the origin and the positive direction before starting a problem on motion? Note, page 56

Because the signs of position, displacement, velocity and acceleration all depend on that choice. In Fig. 4.5 the origin was taken at the ground, while in Fig. 4.10 it was taken where the object was dropped and downwards was chosen as positive. Both are correct, but the numbers in the equation take different signs. If you change the choice in the middle of a solution, the answers will contradict each other. So choose conveniently, write it down, and then keep it fixed till the end.
