Lesson · The Three Kinematic Equations

Topic 8 · where the equations come from

Graphs are visual; equations are exact. For the special case of motion in a straight line with constant acceleration, five quantities are involved — displacement (s), time interval (t), initial velocity (u), final velocity (v) and acceleration (a). Three simple equations connect them.

Derivation 1: v = u + at

Start from the definition of average acceleration: a = (v − u) ÷ t. Multiply both sides by t: at = v − u, and therefore

v = u + at

This equation gives the velocity at any time t if the initial velocity u and the acceleration a are known. On a velocity–time graph it is a straight line whose slope is a.

Derivation 2: s = ut + ½at²

On the velocity–time graph of a body with initial velocity u and final velocity v after time t, the displacement is the area under the line. This area is the rectangle OACD plus the triangle ABC:

rectangle OACD = u × t, and triangle ABC = ½ × base × height = ½ × t × (v − u).

So s = ut + ½t(v − u). Put (v − u) = at from the first equation:

s = ut + ½t × at = ut + ½at²

Derivation 3: v² = u² + 2as

From v = u + at we get t = (v − u) ÷ a. Substitute this value of t in s = ut + ½at²:

s = u × (v − u)/a + ½a × (v − u)²/a²

Multiply throughout by 2a and simplify: 2as = 2u(v − u) + (v − u)² = v² − u².

Therefore v² = u² + 2as. This equation is the most useful one when the time is not given and not asked for.

Which equation to use? Look at what is given and what is asked, and pick the equation that contains exactly those quantities. Time not involved → use v² = u² + 2as. Distance not involved → use v = u + at.
Note: These equations are valid only for constant acceleration. For motion in a straight line in one direction, the distance travelled equals the magnitude of the displacement and the speed equals the magnitude of the velocity. When the motion can be in both directions, the signs of u, v, a and s in the equations tell us the direction of each quantity.

Example: a car starting from rest

A car starts from rest with a constant acceleration of 4 m s⁻². Find its velocity and the distance covered in 5 s.

Solution. u = 0, a = 4 m s⁻², t = 5 s.

v = u + at = 0 + 4 × 5 = 20 m s⁻¹.

s = ut + ½at² = 0 + ½ × 4 × 5² = ½ × 4 × 25 = 50 m.

Check with the third equation: v² = u² + 2as → 20² = 0 + 2 × 4 × 50 = 400 m² s⁻², and √400 = 20 m s⁻¹. ✔

Ask and Understand

Choosing the right equation and using signs correctly.

Q1Why are these equations called kinematic equations?

“Kinematics” is the branch of mechanics that describes motion without asking what causes it. These three equations connect the measurable quantities of motion — initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, time and displacement — without mentioning force or mass. They let us predict where an object will be and how fast it will be moving at a later time, purely from the way the motion started.

Q2How do I decide which of the three equations to use in a problem?

Write down the five quantities u, v, a, t and s, and tick those that are given and that are asked for. Then choose the equation that contains exactly those. If time is neither given nor asked, use v² = u² + 2as. If displacement is not involved, use v = u + at. If the final velocity is not involved, use s = ut + ½at². The remaining equation is then used as a check on the answer, as we did in the worked example.

Q3What happens to the equations when the acceleration is zero?

They reduce to uniform motion. With a = 0, v = u + at becomes v = u, that is, the velocity stays constant. s = ut + ½at² becomes s = ut, and v² = u² + 2as becomes v = u. The distance travelled is then simply speed × time. So uniform motion is just the special case of the kinematic equations with zero acceleration.

Q4Can we use these equations for a ball thrown up and then falling back?

Yes, because for a freely falling body the acceleration is constant (g = 9.8 m s⁻² downwards) throughout — both while the ball goes up and while it comes down. Take the upward direction as positive; then a = −9.8 m s⁻² and the equations work for the whole journey. Going up, the velocity decreases to zero at the highest point; coming down, it grows again in the negative direction. The same three equations can be used, with the correct signs.

Book Questions — Explained

1. Derive the remaining two equations from the two primary equations: (i) s = vt − ½at² and (ii) s = ½(u + v)t. The Journey Beyond

(i) Take s = ut + ½at² and substitute u = v − at (from v = u + at): s = (v − at)t + ½at² = vt − at² + ½at² = vt − ½at².

(ii) The displacement is the area under a velocity–time graph, which for a line rising from u to v in time t is a trapezium: area = ½ × (sum of parallel sides) × height = ½(u + v)t. This is the same formula you use in mathematics for the area of a trapezium.

2. A racing car has a uniform acceleration of 4 m s⁻². What distance will it cover in 10 s after starting? Numerical

Given a = 4 m s⁻², t = 10 s and u = 0 (it starts from rest). Use the second equation: s = ut + ½at² = 0 + ½ × 4 × 10² = 2 × 100 = 200 m. The car covers 200 m in 10 s.

3. Why must the acceleration be constant for these equations to be valid? What if it is not? Note, page 65

The derivations use a single value of acceleration — the slope of the velocity–time line and the area under it are calculated assuming a straight line. If the acceleration keeps changing, the velocity–time graph is a curve, and a single value of a cannot describe the whole interval. In that case the motion has to be broken into small intervals in which the acceleration is nearly constant, or handled by more advanced methods learned in higher grades. In this chapter we consider only the cases where the acceleration is constant.
