Lesson · Distance Travelled and Displacement

Topic 2 · two different ways of measuring a journey

Suppose an athlete starts from O at t = 0 s, reaches B (40 m) at t = 4 s, reaches A (100 m) at t = 10 s, and then runs back along the same path to B, reaching there at t = 16 s.

Total distance travelled between the start and the stop is OA + AB = 100 m + 60 m = 160 m — we simply add up all the ground she covered, in whichever direction she ran.

But the distance between her starting position and her stopping position is only OB = 40 m. That single straight-line change of position is a different physical quantity, called displacement.

Displacement

Displacement is the net change in the position of an object between two given instants of time. Its magnitude is the distance between the positions of the object at those two instants, and its direction is from the position at the first instant towards the position at the second instant.

So displacement = final position − initial position. In the athlete's journey from O to A the displacement is +100 m (positive direction); from A back to B it is −60 m; and for the whole journey from O at t = 0 s to B at t = 16 s it is +40 m in the positive direction.

Displacementdisplacement = final position − initial positionThe SI unit of both distance and displacement is the metre (m).
QuantityWhat it isNeeds direction?Can it be negative?Can it be zero?

DisplacementNet change in positionYesYes, for motion in the negative directionYes, if the object returns to its starting point
Distance is a scalar; displacement is a vector.

When are they equal, and when are they very different?

Equal: for motion in a straight line, the total distance travelled and the magnitude of displacement are equal if the object moves without turning back, that is, if it keeps moving in one direction only.

Different: if the object turns back, the distance keeps growing while the net change in position becomes smaller. If it comes back to the starting point, the distance is non-zero but the displacement is zero.

Example: round trip on a circular track

An athlete runs one complete round of a circular track of radius 7 m. The distance travelled is the circumference = 2πR ≈ 2 × (22/7) × 7 = 44 m. But the athlete is back at the starting point, so the displacement is 0 m.

Note: For motion in a straight line, the magnitude of displacement can never be greater than the total distance travelled. It is at most equal to it. So statement (iii) of Activity 4.1 — “its magnitude is less than or equal to the total distance travelled” — is the correct one.

Why does this matter in real life?

The fuel used up by a vehicle depends on the total distance travelled, not on the displacement — the engine burns petrol for every metre of the path, in whatever direction the vehicle moves. If you drive 5 km to a shop and come back 5 km home, the displacement is zero but 10 km of fuel has been burnt.

Ask and Understand

Distances, signs, path lengths and why the difference matters.

Q1Why can two journeys with the same starting and ending points have different displacements?

They cannot. Displacement depends only on the initial and final positions, so any two journeys between the same two points have exactly the same displacement — a straight line from the start to the finish, with a direction. What can differ is the distance travelled: a winding path is longer than a straight one. This is why displacement is called the shortest distance between two positions, while distance is the actual path length.

Q2Can the magnitude of displacement ever be greater than the distance travelled?

No, never. The straight line joining the initial and final positions is the shortest path between them, so its length cannot exceed the length of the actual path followed. So magnitude of displacement ≤ total distance travelled, in every possible case. They are equal when the object moves along a straight line without reversing its direction.

Q3Which of the two — distance or displacement — is used to calculate the fuel consumed, and why?

Distance travelled is used. Fuel is burnt by the engine for every metre the vehicle moves, regardless of the direction of motion, so the total path length decides the consumption. Displacement only tells us the net change of position and can even be zero while a lot of fuel has been used — for example a car going to a market and returning home has zero displacement but has burnt fuel for the full to-and-fro distance.

Q4Distance and displacement have the same unit. Why are they still different quantities?

Because they are defined differently and carry different information. Both are measured in metres, but distance is the total path length (a scalar with only a numerical value), while displacement is the net change in position and needs a direction as well (a vector). Two quantities can share a unit and still be different physical quantities — for example, torque and energy both use N m. In every calculation you must ask whether the question wants the total path length or the net change in position.

2. Which statement is true about displacement? (i) It is never zero. (ii) Its magnitude can be greater than the total distance travelled. (iii) Its magnitude is less than or equal to the total distance travelled. (iv) Its magnitude is always less than the distance travelled. Activity 4.1

Statement (iii) is correct. Displacement can be zero (when the object returns to the start), so (i) is wrong. Its magnitude can never exceed the distance travelled, so (ii) is wrong. Statement (iv) is also wrong because in one-direction motion the two are equal, not strictly less. Hence magnitude of displacement ≤ total distance travelled.

3. In the athlete's journey O → B → A → B, when will the displacement be zero and what will the total distance travelled be in that case? Pause and Ponder

The displacement is zero whenever the athlete finishes at the reference point O. For example, if she runs from O to A (100 m) and then back to O, her displacement is 0 m while the total distance travelled is 100 m + 100 m = 200 m. In general, for any journey that ends where it began, displacement is zero and the distance is twice the length of one-way path (or the whole path length travelled).
