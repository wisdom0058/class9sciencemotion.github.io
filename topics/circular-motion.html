Lesson · Motion in a Plane and Uniform Circular Motion

Topic 10 · constant speed, changing direction

So far we have studied motion along a straight line — motion in one dimension. Now let us look at motion in a plane, which is motion in two dimensions: a vehicle overtaking another, the path of a kicked ball, a satellite going round the Earth, a child on a merry-go-round.

Circular motion and uniform circular motion

Circular motion: motion of an object along a circular path.

Uniform circular motion: motion along a circular path with constant speed.

Distance and displacement in circular motion

A child on a merry-go-round moves from A to B to C along the curve. The distance travelled is the length of the curve ABC, while the displacement is the straight line AC — the two are not equal.

In one complete revolution, the distance travelled is the circumference and the displacement is zero, because the child returns to the starting point.

If the radius of the circular path is R, the distance covered in one revolution is 2πR. If the object takes time T for one revolution, its average speed is 2πR ÷ T, while its average velocity for that interval is 0, since the displacement is zero.

Uniform circular motionaverage speed = 2πR ÷ TR = radius of the circle, T = time period, the time for one complete revolution.

Example: a merry-go-round

A child on a merry-go-round moves on a circle of radius 3 m and completes one round in 12 s. Find the distance travelled and the average speed for one round, and the average velocity.

Solution. Distance = 2πR = 2 × (22/7) × 3 ≈ 18.9 m. Average speed = 18.9 ÷ 12 = 1.57 m s⁻¹. The child comes back to the same point, so the displacement is zero and hence the average velocity is 0 m s⁻¹.

Why uniform circular motion is accelerated motion

The speed is constant at every point on the circle, but the direction of the velocity keeps changing. Velocity is a vector, so a change in direction is a change in velocity — and a change in velocity means acceleration. Therefore uniform circular motion is an accelerated motion, even though the speed does not change.

The velocity at any point is along the tangent to the circle at that point, in the direction of motion. A tangent is a straight line that meets the circle at one and only one point.

From a polygon to a circle

An athlete running along a rectangular track changes direction 4 times in one round. On a hexagonal track the direction changes 6 times. If the number of sides keeps increasing indefinitely, the turns become more and more frequent, each side shrinks to a point, and the track approaches a circle — in which the direction of velocity changes continuously.

Activity 4.5: the marble inside a ring

Place an adhesive-tape ring flat on a smooth surface and throw a marble so that it rolls along the inner boundary. While the ring is in place the marble moves along the circle. Now lift the ring: the marble at once moves off along a straight line — the tangent at the point where it was released. It continues in the direction it had at that instant, which is exactly what the tangent picture predicts.

Note: In real life the conditions for uniform circular motion — constant speed and a perfectly circular path — are seldom met exactly, so uniform circular motion is an idealised model. Even so it is extremely useful: it is the foundation for describing planets going round the Sun and a vehicle taking a circular turn.

Ask and Understand

Tangent, time period and why speed is not the whole story.

Q1If the speed is constant, how can the motion be accelerated?

Because acceleration depends on the change in velocity, and velocity has both magnitude and direction. In uniform circular motion the magnitude (the speed) stays the same, but the direction changes at every instant as the object turns around the circle. A change in direction is a change in velocity, so there is an acceleration. In everyday life we often say “accelerating” only when a vehicle speeds up, but a vehicle taking a turn at a steady speed is also accelerating.

Q2What is the time period of circular motion and how is it related to speed?

The time period T is the time taken to complete one revolution. In that time the object covers the whole circumference 2πR at a constant speed v, so v = 2πR ÷ T, or T = 2πR ÷ v. For example, a child on a merry-go-round on a circle of radius 3 m moving at 1.57 m s⁻¹ takes 18.9 ÷ 1.57 ≈ 12 s for one round. A larger radius or a smaller speed means a longer time period.

Q3Why does the marble move in a straight line when the ring is lifted?

Because at every instant the marble is moving along the tangent to the circle at its position. The ring is what keeps pushing it and bending its path into a circle. Once the ring is removed, nothing bends the path any more, so the marble continues in the direction it already had — that is, along the straight tangent line. This shows that the velocity at any point of circular motion is along the tangent at that point.

Q4Give some examples of uniform circular motion from daily life, and say when the model fails.

Examples: the tip of a clock's minute hand, a child on a merry-go-round moving at a steady speed, a satellite in a nearly circular orbit, the blades of a fan, a vehicle taking a well-banked circular turn at a constant speed. The model fails when the speed is not really constant or the path is not exactly a circle — for example a car slowing down as it turns, or an athlete on a running track whose speed varies through the race. Real motions are therefore described as approximately uniform circular motion. Idealised models like this are still valuable, because they are the base on which the more complicated real cases are built.

Book Questions — Explained

1. Activity 4.5: A marble is thrown inside a ring so that it rolls along the inner boundary. What happens when the ring is lifted while the marble is moving? Activity 4.5

The marble leaves the circular path and moves off in a straight line, along the tangent at the point where it was when the ring was lifted. It does not continue to curve, because the ring was the thing forcing it to turn. This tells us that the direction of the velocity of an object in circular motion is along the tangent to the circle at that instant.

2. Take a cardboard disc with numbers 1 to 12 on the outer part (7 cm from the centre) and the letters ABCDEF on the inner part (4 cm from the centre). Spin it slowly and then faster. Why do the numbers fade while the letters remain visible? The Journey Beyond

Both the numbers and the letters go round the same angular rate, but the numbers are farther from the centre, so they travel a larger distance in the same time — their speed is greater (v = 2πR ÷ T is larger when R is larger). At high speed the eye cannot follow the outer marks separately and they blur into a smear, which is why the numbers fade or disappear while the slower inner letters remain readable. So the speeds of the numbers and the letters are not the same — the outer ones move faster.

3. A smartphone accelerometer shows different readings when the phone is on an outstretched palm and when it is kept on the floor. What does this tell us? The Journey Beyond

On the floor the phone is at rest, so its readings stay steady. On an outstretched palm the hand is never perfectly still — tiny involuntary movements make the readings keep changing. This shows that even in “rest” situations there are very small accelerations, and that real motion is more complicated than the idealised models in the textbook. Such tiny movements are also studied in medical research, for example in movement disorders.
