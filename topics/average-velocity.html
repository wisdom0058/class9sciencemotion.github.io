Lesson · Average Velocity and Rate of Change

Topic 4 · speed with a direction

Speed tells us how fast an object moves, but it says nothing about the direction of motion. In many situations the direction matters as much as the speed — a bus going north and a bus going south at the same speed behave very differently. So we define a new quantity that describes how fast the position is changing and in which direction.

Average velocity

Average velocity is the change in position (displacement) divided by the time interval in which the change occurs.

Unlike speed, average velocity needs a magnitude and a direction. For motion in a straight line, the direction is shown by a plus or minus sign: positive means motion in the positive direction, negative means motion in the negative direction.

Average velocityaverage velocity = displacement ÷ time intervalSI unit: m s⁻¹ (same as speed), also km h⁻¹. If vₐᵥ is average velocity, s is displacement and t is the time interval, then average velocity = s ÷ t.

Rate of change

To describe how fast or slow any physical quantity changes, we use the idea of a rate of change: the ratio of a change in one quantity to the corresponding change in time. Average velocity is therefore the average rate of change of position with respect to time.

The same idea will be used in the next topic for velocity: average acceleration is the rate of change of velocity.

Note: Over a long interval, average velocity can be very different from the velocity at a particular instant. The velocity at an instant is called instantaneous velocity; it is what the speedometer shows. In this chapter, when we simply say ‘velocity’, we mean the velocity of the object at a particular instant.

Example 4.2 again — Sarang's swim

Sarang swims 25 m to the far end and back in 50 s. Total distance = 50 m, displacement = 0 m. Average speed = 50 ÷ 50 = 1 m s⁻¹, but average velocity = 0 ÷ 50 = 0 m s⁻¹. The swimmer was certainly moving — his average velocity for the round trip is zero because his net change of position is zero.

Example: a bus going and coming back

A bus leaves the bus stand, goes 30 km north in 40 minutes, waits 10 minutes, and comes back 30 km south in 40 minutes. Distance = 60 km, displacement = 0 km, total time = 90 min = 1.5 h. Average speed = 60 ÷ 1.5 = 40 km h⁻¹; average velocity = 0 ÷ 1.5 = 0 km h⁻¹.

Ask and Understand

Direction, signs and the pool example.

Q1What exactly is the difference between average speed and average velocity?

Average speed uses the total distance travelled, and gives only a numerical value (it is a scalar). Average velocity uses the displacement, and gives both a magnitude and a direction (it is a vector). So a runner who completes one round of a track in 100 s has an average speed of (circumference ÷ 100) m s⁻¹ but an average velocity of 0 m s⁻¹. In one-direction straight-line motion the two are numerically equal, and only then.

Q2Can average velocity be negative? What does the sign mean?

Yes. Average velocity can be positive, negative or zero depending on the direction of the net displacement. We first choose which direction along the line is positive — say east. If the object ends up west of its starting point, its displacement is negative, and dividing a negative displacement by a positive time gives a negative average velocity. The minus sign is not a “smaller” speed; it only says that the motion is in the negative direction. Average speed, being based on distance, can never be negative.

Q3What is meant by instantaneous velocity and why is it needed?

Instantaneous velocity is the velocity of the object at one particular instant. It is found by taking the average velocity over smaller and smaller time intervals around that instant, until the value settles at a fixed number. It is needed because average velocity can hide a lot of detail: a car that runs 60 km in one hour has an average velocity of 60 km h⁻¹, but the speedometer shows that its instantaneous velocity was changing all the time. A speedometer reading is a good everyday example of instantaneous speed, and the direction of the tyres gives the direction of instantaneous velocity.

Q4Why is the swimming-pool example important for understanding velocity?

Because it separates the two ideas cleanly. Sarang really does swim 50 m in 50 s, so his average speed is 1 m s⁻¹ and it would be wrong to say he did not move. But he ends exactly where he started, so his net change of position is zero, and his average velocity for that interval is 0 m s⁻¹. The example shows that a zero average velocity does not mean the object was at rest — it only means that the displacement over that interval was zero.

Book Questions — Explained

1. Trace the steps for finding the average velocity of a moving object from its position–time graph between two instants t₁ and t₂. Concept check

(i) Locate t₁ and t₂ on the time axis. (ii) Go vertically up from each to meet the graph at points A and B. (iii) Read the corresponding positions s₁ and s₂ from the position axis. (iv) Average velocity = (s₂ − s₁) ÷ (t₂ − t₁). Geometrically this is the slope of the line AB on the position–time graph, which is why slope is said to give velocity.

2. A car moves with a constant speed of 20 m s⁻¹ along a straight road and then turns back with the same speed. Compare its average speed and average velocity for the whole journey. Numerical

Suppose the car goes distance d one way and comes back the same d. Total distance = 2d and displacement = 0. So the average speed = 2d ÷ t, a non-zero value, while the average velocity = 0 ÷ t = 0 m s⁻¹. If, instead, the car goes d and stops, both average speed and the magnitude of average velocity equal d ÷ t. So turning back is exactly the situation in which the two quantities differ.

3. A graph shows the position of two objects A and B against time. How can we tell which has the larger magnitude of average velocity? Example 4.7

Compare the slopes. For the same time interval on the time axis, draw lines parallel to the axes and compare the displacements: the object whose position changes by a larger amount in the same interval has the larger average velocity. In Fig. 4.16 the line for B is steeper than that for A, so B has the greater magnitude of average velocity.
