Lesson · Motion, Rest and the Reference Point

Topic 1 · the starting point of the whole chapter

Look around you. A butterfly flits from flower to flower, a snake slithers, a hare hops, horses gallop, tendrils of a climber wind around a support, a flytrap snaps shut, dust particles dance in a sunbeam, ocean tides rise and fall and clouds gather in the sky. Everything in nature is in motion — from huge stars to tiny subatomic particles.

Motion around us is complicated, so scientists first study motion in its idealised, simplified forms. You have already met three simple kinds of motion: linear (in a straight line), circular and oscillatory. In this chapter we study the simplest kind of all — motion in a straight line, also called linear motion — and then uniform circular motion.

Motion in a straight line

An object moving along a straight path is said to be in linear motion. Examples: children racing in a swimming lane, a ball falling vertically, a car on a straight stretch of highway, a train on a straight track, a swimmer swimming along one lane.

Because the object can move in only one of two directions here (forward or backward), this is the simplest motion to describe. We can describe it with words, with numbers, with equations and with graphs.

How do we describe the position of an object?

To talk about the motion of an object, we first need to say where it is at different instants of time. For that we choose a fixed point called the reference point, and then give the distance of the object from it and the direction in which the object lies. Distance and direction together describe the position of the object at that instant.

Example: Neena on a straight track

Neena runs on a straight track. We take her starting point as the reference point and mark it O (the origin) on a straight line with distances marked on it. She starts from O, and her positions at two later instants are marked as B (40 m) and A (100 m). To describe these positions completely we also need the direction, so along a straight line we use signs: positions to the right of O are positive and positions to the left of O are negative. So B is at +40 m and A is at +100 m.

When is an object in motion, and when is it at rest?

In motion: if the position of the object with respect to the reference point changes with time.

At rest: if its position with respect to the reference point does not change with time.

Notice that both statements contain the words with respect to the reference point. A girl sitting inside a moving bus is at rest with respect to the bus, but she is in motion with respect to the road. The same object can be at rest for one observer and in motion for another — so motion and rest are always described relative to a chosen reference point.

Note: An instant of time is a single reading of a clock (for example t = 4 s). A time interval is the duration between two instants (for example from t = 4 s to t = 10 s, an interval of 6 s). Do not mix the two.

Some useful words

Magnitude means the numerical value together with its unit. So the magnitude of a position may be 40 m. A quantity that is completely described by its magnitude alone is called a scalar (distance, time, mass). A quantity that needs both magnitude and direction is called a vector (displacement, velocity, acceleration).

Choose the origin and the positive direction wherever it makes the problem easy — but once chosen, do not change it in the middle of a problem.

Ask and Understand

Four ideas students ask about most in this topic.

Q1What is a reference point, and why must we choose one?

A reference point is a fixed point chosen by us, usually marked O (the origin), from which we measure where an object is. Without it, a statement like “the ball is at 20 m” has no meaning — 20 m from where? Once the reference point is fixed, we can describe the position of the object by giving the distance from that point and the direction in which the object lies. All measurements of position, displacement, velocity and acceleration in a problem are made with respect to the reference point we chose.

Q2Why can the same object be at rest for one person and in motion for another?

Because both motion and rest are defined with respect to a chosen reference point. A passenger inside a moving train is at rest with respect to the train (her position inside the coach does not change), but she is in motion with respect to the platform or the ground. Similarly, an object kept inside your room is at rest with respect to the room, yet it is travelling with the Earth around the Sun. So there is no absolute rest or absolute motion — only rest or motion relative to the reference point we choose.

Q3What is the difference between an instant of time and a time interval?

An instant of time is a single reading of a clock, for example t = 5 s. It is just one point of time with no duration. A time interval is the duration between two instants, for example the interval from t = 5 s to t = 12 s, which equals 12 s − 5 s = 7 s. We write it as Δt. All the quantities in this chapter — speed, velocity and acceleration — are calculated by dividing a change by a time interval, never by an instant.

Q4Why do scientists study idealised, simplified forms of motion first?

Real motion is very complicated: a bird flying in the wind, wind-blown leaves or traffic on a busy road have many things changing at once. If we try to study everything together, no clear pattern appears. So scientists first study simplified forms such as straight-line (linear) motion at constant speed or with constant acceleration, understand them thoroughly, and then use these results as a base for describing more complex real motions. Straight-line motion is the simplest of all, so this chapter starts there.

Book Questions — Explained

1. A ball is thrown vertically upwards from O. It rises straight to B and then falls back to O. Can this be called motion in a straight line? Activity 4.1

Yes. Up and down are along the same vertical line, so the ball never leaves the straight path — it only changes direction of travel along that line. Such motion along a single straight line is called motion in a straight line (one-dimensional motion). We take one direction as positive (say upward) and the opposite direction as negative, and then the position of the ball can be written as a number with a sign at every instant.

2. A ball rolls down an inclined track and passes points A, B, C and D. Is its motion a straight line motion? Pause and Ponder

The track is inclined but it is still straight, and the ball moves along that same straight line, so yes — it is motion in a straight line. However, it cannot be shown on the horizontal line of Fig. 4.3 exactly as it is, because that figure represents a horizontal road; for an inclined track we would draw the line along the incline. The values of total distance travelled and magnitude of displacement from O at A, B, C and D are equal, because the ball keeps moving forward in one direction and never turns back.

3. Why is distance travelled not enough to describe a journey completely? Concept check

Distance tells us only how much ground was covered; it says nothing about direction. Two journeys can cover exactly the same distance but end in completely different places. That is why we need direction as well: the pair of distance and direction gives position, displacement and velocity. This is the reason for the rule that for a complete description we must state the reference point, the distance and the direction.
