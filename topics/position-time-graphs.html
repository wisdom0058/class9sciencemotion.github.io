Lesson · Plotting Graphs and Position–Time Graphs

Topic 6 · turning motion into a picture

Graphs are one of the most powerful ways of describing motion. A graph shows at a glance how position, velocity or acceleration change with time, and it makes it easy to compare two objects.

How to plot a graph

On graph paper, draw two perpendicular lines. Their intersection is the origin O. The horizontal line OX is the x-axis and the vertical line OY is the y-axis.

  1. Decide which quantity goes on which axis. For motion data, we usually put time on the x-axis and position (or velocity) on the y-axis.
  2. Choose a suitable scale for each axis so that the whole data fits the paper comfortably — for example, on the x-axis 5 divisions = 1 s and on the y-axis 5 divisions = 20 m.
  3. Mark the values along each axis from the origin.
  4. For each pair of values from the data table, plot a point, then join the points smoothly.

Reading motion from a position–time graph

Straight line, slanting: the object moves with a constant velocity — equal changes of position in equal intervals of time.

Curved line: the velocity is not constant, so the object is in accelerated motion.

Horizontal line (parallel to the time axis): the position does not change with time, so the object is at rest.

Steeper line: a bigger change of position in the same time, so a larger velocity. Comparing two lines on the same graph therefore compares their velocities.

Slope gives velocity

Take two points A and B on the graph. From A and B draw lines parallel to the axes; this forms a right-angled triangle. The vertical side BC represents the change in position (s₂ − s₁) and the horizontal side CA represents the change in time (t₂ − t₁). Then

average velocity = (s₂ − s₁) ÷ (t₂ − t₁)

This ratio is called the slope of the line AB. Slope measures the steepness of the line, and it tells us the rate of change of the quantity on the y-axis with respect to the quantity on the x-axis. So on a position–time graph, slope = velocity.

Example 4.7: comparing two objects

Figure 4.16a shows the position–time graphs of two objects A and B. Which has the larger magnitude of average velocity?

Solution. Draw lines parallel to the axes for the same time interval. The displacement of B is greater than that of A in the same interval, so the slope of B's line is steeper. Hence B has the larger velocity.

A graph is only as good as the scale you choose. If the scales are badly chosen, the data may cram into a corner or run off the paper. Experiments with different scales show that scales should use the available space well and be easy to read — for example, 1 division = 1 unit is much easier than 1 division = 0.37 unit.

Ask and Understand

Scales, slopes and what a graph can and cannot tell you.

Q1Why do we plot time on the x-axis and position on the y-axis?

By convention the quantity that we choose freely and that always moves forward — time — is taken on the horizontal axis, and the quantity that responds to it is taken on the vertical axis. This makes reading easy: go up from any time on the x-axis to the graph, then across to the y-axis, and you have the position at that instant. Graph paper with time on the horizontal axis also matches how we read a clock moving forward.

Q2How do we choose a good scale for a graph?

Choose scales so that the largest value of each quantity fits comfortably on the sheet and the smallest change you need to read is still visible. Easy multiples are best — for example 5 divisions = 1 s, or 1 division = 10 m. The two axes need not have the same scale. Always write the scale on the graph. A badly chosen scale either crowds all the points into a corner or wastes most of the paper, and in both cases the graph becomes hard to read.

Q3What does the slope of a graph tell us in general?

Slope is the change in the quantity on the y-axis divided by the corresponding change in the quantity on the x-axis. It is the rate of change of the vertical quantity with respect to the horizontal one. So on a position–time graph, slope gives velocity; on a velocity–time graph, slope gives acceleration. A steeper line means a faster rate of change, a horizontal line means zero rate of change, and a line sloping the other way means a negative rate of change.

Q4Can we find the distance travelled from a position–time graph?

If the object moves in one direction only, then yes: the distance travelled equals the change in position, which we read from the graph. But if the object turns back, the graph shows only the net change of position (the displacement). For example, a graph that rises to 100 m, then falls back to 40 m, shows a displacement of +40 m although the distance travelled was 160 m. So to get distance from such a graph you must add up the lengths of all the rising and falling portions separately.

Book Questions — Explained

1. Describe the steps of Activity 4.3 for plotting the position–time graph of the vehicle whose data are given in Table 4.3. Activity 4.3

(i) Take graph paper and draw the perpendicular axes, marking the origin O, the x-axis OX and the y-axis OY. (ii) Put time on the x-axis and position on the y-axis. (iii) Choose the scales, e.g. 5 divisions = 1 s on the x-axis and 5 divisions = 20 m on the y-axis. (iv) Mark the values 1 s, 2 s, 3 s … and 20 m, 40 m, 60 m … along the axes. (v) Plot each pair of values from the table as a point — (0,0) lies at the origin itself. (vi) Join the points with a straight line. The result is a straight line through the origin, showing that the vehicle has a constant velocity of 20 m s⁻¹.

2. Example 4.6: What does the graph in Fig. 4.15 indicate about the nature of motion of the vehicle, where the line stays at 40 m? Example 4.6

The position of the vehicle is 40 m from the origin and does not change with time, so the vehicle is at rest at a distance of 40 m from the origin. On a position–time graph a straight line parallel to the time axis always represents a stationary object. Note that because the position is not zero at t = 0, this position–time graph is not the same as a distance–time graph.

3. Example 4.7: The position–time graphs of two objects A and B are given. Which object has the higher magnitude of average velocity, and how do you decide? Example 4.7

Draw lines from each graph parallel to the axes for the same time interval, as in Fig. 4.16b. The displacement of B in that interval is more than the displacement of A, so the slope of B's line is steeper than A's. Since slope = velocity on a position–time graph, the velocity of B is higher than that of A.
