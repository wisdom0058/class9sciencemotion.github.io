Lesson · Average Speed and Uniform Motion

Topic 3 · how fast an object moves

Motion means a change of position. But how do we say how fast or how slow an object is moving? For that we compare the distance covered with the time taken.

Average speed

Average speed is the total distance travelled divided by the time interval during which this distance is covered.

Distance travelled has no direction (it is a scalar), so average speed also has no direction — it is a number with a unit, and it is never negative.

Average speedaverage speed = total distance travelled ÷ time intervalSI unit: m s⁻¹ (metre per second). Also commonly used: km h⁻¹.

Uniform and non-uniform motion in a straight line

Uniform motion: the object travels equal distances in equal intervals of time, for every possible choice of time interval. Then its speed is constant.

Non-uniform motion: the object travels unequal distances in equal intervals of time. Then its speed is increasing, decreasing, or a mixture of both. If the distances covered in successive equal intervals keep increasing, the speed is increasing.

Example 4.1 (India's Scientific Contributions): two postmen

The problem, from the Ganitakaumudi (14th century CE): two postmen start walking towards each other from a distance of 210 yojanas. One walks 9 yojanas per day and the other 5 yojanas per day. In how many days will they meet?

Solution. In one day the two postmen together cover 9 + 5 = 14 yojanas. To meet, they must together cover the whole 210 yojanas. So the time needed is 210 ÷ 14 = 15 days. (Check: in 15 days the first covers 15 × 9 = 135 yojanas and the second 15 × 5 = 75 yojanas, and 135 + 75 = 210 yojanas.)

Note: The idea that speed is distance divided by time was known in India long ago — it is found in the Aryabhatiya of Aryabhata (5th century CE), and the problem above comes from the Ganitakaumudi of Narayana Pandita (14th century CE).

Example 4.2: Sarang's swim

Sarang takes 50 s to swim from one end of a 25 m pool to the other end and back. Find his average speed and his average velocity for the 50 s interval.

Solution. Total distance travelled = 25 m + 25 m = 50 m; time = 50 s. Average speed = 50 ÷ 50 = 1 m s⁻¹. He finishes at the same end where he started, so the displacement = 0 m and therefore the average velocity = 0 ÷ 50 = 0 m s⁻¹.

If a body moves with speed v₁ for time t₁ and speed v₂ for time t₂, the average speed is (v₁t₁ + v₂t₂) ÷ (t₁ + t₂) — not (v₁ + v₂) ÷ 2. Average speed is always worked out from total distance and total time.

Ask and Understand

Speed, averages and the uniform / non-uniform difference.

Q1Why is average speed called “average”? Why not just speed?

Because in non-uniform motion the speed keeps changing from instant to instant. A bus may move fast on a clear road and slow in a traffic jam, so a single value of speed cannot describe the whole journey. The average speed flattens the whole trip into one number: “if the bus had moved at one steady speed for the whole time, that steady speed would have been this value”. It tells us the overall rate of covering distance, not what the speedometer showed at each instant.

Q2A car travels from A to B at 60 km h⁻¹ and returns from B to A at 40 km h⁻¹. What is its average speed for the whole journey, and why is it not 50 km h⁻¹?

Let the distance AB be d km. Time going = d/60 h, time returning = d/40 h. Total time = d/60 + d/40 = (2d + 3d)/120 = 5d/120 h, and total distance = 2d km. So average speed = 2d ÷ (5d/120) = 48 km h⁻¹, not 50. The simple average 50 fails because the car spends more time moving at the slower speed. That is why average speed is always computed as total distance ÷ total time.

Q3What is the difference between uniform and non-uniform motion?

In uniform motion in a straight line an object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time, so its speed is constant — for example a train moving on a straight track at a steady 60 km h⁻¹. In non-uniform motion it covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time, so its speed is changing — for example a car in city traffic. Uniform motion is an idealised case; most real motions are non-uniform. Notice that a body moving with constant speed on a curved path is not in uniform motion in a straight line, because its direction is changing.

Q4If the average speed of a journey is 40 km h⁻¹, does it mean the vehicle moved at exactly 40 km h⁻¹ all the time?

No. Average speed is the overall value for the whole interval; the actual speed may have been higher in some parts and lower in others, and zero while the vehicle was stopped at a signal. For example, a bus that runs 40 km in one hour with two five-minute stops has an average speed of 40 km h⁻¹, although its speedometer stayed above 40 km h⁻¹ while it was moving. Average speed is useful for planning journeys, but not for describing what happened at a particular instant.

Book Questions — Explained

1. Example 4.1: Two postmen start walking towards each other from a distance of 210 yojanas — one at 9 yojanas per day, the other at 5 yojanas per day. In how many days will they meet? India's Scientific Contributions

Together they cover 9 + 5 = 14 yojanas per day, and they have to cover 210 yojanas together. Time = 210 ÷ 14 = 15 days. In those 15 days the faster postman covers 135 yojanas and the slower one 75 yojanas, which add up to 210 yojanas. This problem comes from the Ganitakaumudi (14th century CE), showing that the relation “speed = distance ÷ time” was used in India long before modern textbooks.

2. Look up the time taken by different cars to go from 0 to 100 km h⁻¹ and calculate the magnitude of average acceleration for each. Activity 4.2

Use a = (v − u) ÷ t after converting the speeds to m s⁻¹. Since 100 km h⁻¹ = 100 × 1000 ÷ 3600 = 27.8 m s⁻¹ and u = 0, we get a = 27.8 ÷ t. So for t = 5 s, a = 5.6 m s⁻²; for t = 10 s, a = 2.8 m s⁻²; for t = 20 s, a = 1.4 m s⁻². A sports car that reaches 100 km h⁻¹ in about 5 s has a much larger acceleration than a family car taking 15–20 s. This is exactly why the “0 to 100” time is quoted for cars.

3. Fuel used up in a vehicle depends on the total distance travelled or on the displacement? Justify. Pause and Ponder

It depends on the total distance travelled. The engine consumes fuel for every metre the vehicle actually moves, whichever direction it moves in. Displacement only measures the net change of position, so it can be zero (a trip to the market and back) even though a lot of fuel was burnt. Since fuel consumption is proportional to the path length, we compare it with distance, not displacement.
