Lesson · Using the Equations: Stopping and Safe Distance

Topic 9 · the chapter's opening question answered

Now we use the kinematic equations on a real problem: stopping a vehicle. This is also the question with which the chapter began — how much distance should we keep from the vehicle ahead?

Example 4.8: how far does a car travel while braking?

A car is moving on a highway and the brakes are applied, causing an acceleration of −4 m s⁻². How far will the car travel before stopping if its velocity when the brakes were applied was (i) 54 km h⁻¹ and (ii) 108 km h⁻¹?

Solution. Given a = −4 m s⁻² and v = 0 m s⁻¹. Time is not given and not asked, so use v² = u² + 2as.

0 = u² + 2(−4)s → 0 = u² − 8s → s = u² ÷ 8.

(i) u = 54 km h⁻¹ = 15 m s⁻¹ → s = 15² ÷ 8 = 225 ÷ 8 = 28.1 m.

(ii) u = 108 km h⁻¹ = 30 m s⁻¹ → s = 30² ÷ 8 = 900 ÷ 8 = 112.5 m.

Notice that doubling the speed made the braking distance four times larger, because u appears as u² in the equation.

Stopping distance = reaction distance + braking distance

A driver does not brake the instant the danger appears — there is a short delay called the reaction time, usually about 0.5 s to 1 s. During that time the vehicle keeps moving at its original speed, covering the reaction distance = u × reaction time.

After the brake is pressed, the vehicle covers the braking distance = u² ÷ (2a), where a is the magnitude of the retardation.

So total stopping distance = u × treaction + u² ÷ 2a.

The total stopping distance grows much faster than the speed — doubling the speed roughly quadruples the braking part.

Why a safe distance must be kept

If the vehicle ahead suddenly brakes, your vehicle also needs its full stopping distance to come to rest. Therefore the distance you keep must be at least equal to your stopping distance — and larger on wet roads, with worn-out tyres, at night, in fog or when the driver is tired, because all these increase the reaction time or reduce the braking capacity.

This is also why vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication technology is being developed in many countries, including India: vehicles exchange signals and warn drivers of a possible collision.

Worked problem in the textbook style

A bus travels at 36 km h⁻¹ when the driver sees an obstacle 30 m ahead. He takes 0.5 s to react before pressing the brake, and the bus then slows down with a constant acceleration of 2.5 m s⁻². Will the bus stop before the obstacle?

Solution. u = 36 km h⁻¹ = 10 m s⁻¹. Reaction distance = 10 × 0.5 = 5 m. Braking distance = u² ÷ 2a = 100 ÷ (2 × 2.5) = 100 ÷ 5 = 20 m. Total stopping distance = 5 + 20 = 25 m, which is less than 30 m. So the bus will stop before the obstacle, with 5 m to spare.

Ask and Understand

Reaction time, braking distance and road safety.

Q1Why is the braking distance proportional to the square of the speed?

Because of the equation v² = u² + 2as. With the final velocity v = 0 this becomes 0 = u² + 2as, so s = −u² ÷ 2a, that is, s = u² ÷ (2a) when a is the magnitude of the retardation. The speed enters as a square, so at double the speed the car has four times as much kinetic “motion to kill” and needs four times the distance. This is why speeding is so dangerous even when the brakes are good.

Q2What is reaction time and why can we not ignore it?

Reaction time is the delay between seeing a hazard and actually pressing the brake — usually 0.5 s to 1 s for an alert driver, and longer when the driver is tired, distracted or under the influence of alcohol. During this time the vehicle continues at its original speed, so it covers a reaction distance u × t. At 72 km h⁻¹ (20 m s⁻¹) even a 0.5 s reaction adds 10 m to the stopping distance — that is two car lengths. Ignoring reaction time gives a stopping distance far smaller than reality.

Q3On what does the braking capacity of a vehicle depend?

The braking capacity is the retardation (negative acceleration) that the brakes can produce. It depends on the condition of the brakes, the tyres and the road surface. On a dry road the tyres grip well and the retardation is large; on a wet road, on loose gravel or with worn-out tyres the grip is poor, so the retardation is smaller and the braking distance u² ÷ 2a becomes larger. A heavily loaded vehicle also needs a longer distance. This is why the safe distance must be increased in rain, fog or snow.

Q4How does the answer to the chapter's opening question follow from this topic?

The chapter opened by asking how much distance we must keep from a truck ahead and whether it depends on speed. The answer is: the distance must be at least your own total stopping distance — reaction distance plus braking distance — and yes, it very much depends on speed. At 36 km h⁻¹ the answer is about 25 m, while at 72 km h⁻¹ it is about 90 m, more than three times as much. This is exactly why the safe gap must grow when you drive faster, and why it must be even larger on a wet road or when you are tired.

Book Questions — Explained

1. How much distance should we maintain from the truck ahead to avoid a collision if it suddenly applies the brakes? Think It Over, page 48

We must keep at least our own total stopping distance, because that is how far our vehicle will travel before it stops. Total stopping distance = u × reaction time + u² ÷ 2a. For a car at 54 km h⁻¹ (15 m s⁻¹), with a reaction time of 0.5 s and a retardation of 4 m s⁻², this is 7.5 m + 225 ÷ 8 = 7.5 + 28.1 = about 36 m. In practice drivers are advised to keep a gap of at least two to three seconds of travel time, which automatically adjusts the distance to the speed.

2. Does this distance depend upon the speed with which we are moving? Think It Over, page 48

Yes, very strongly. The reaction distance grows in proportion to the speed (u × t), while the braking distance grows with the square of the speed (u² ÷ 2a). So at double the speed the reaction distance doubles but the braking distance becomes four times larger, and the total stopping distance becomes much more than double. That is why a safe gap must be increased sharply as speed rises.

3. Example 4.8: Brakes produce an acceleration of −4 m s⁻². How far will the car travel before stopping if it was moving at (i) 54 km h⁻¹ and (ii) 108 km h⁻¹? Example 4.8

Use v² = u² + 2as with v = 0 and a = −4 m s⁻², which gives s = u² ÷ 8.

(i) u = 54 km h⁻¹ = 15 m s⁻¹ → s = 225 ÷ 8 = 28.1 m.

(ii) u = 108 km h⁻¹ = 30 m s⁻¹ → s = 900 ÷ 8 = 112.5 m.

The answers show the strong effect of speed: doubling the speed makes the stopping distance four times larger.
