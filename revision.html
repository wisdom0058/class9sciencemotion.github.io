At a Glance

  • The distance and direction of an object with respect to the reference point, at any instant of time, describe the position of the object at that instant.
  • If the position of the object with respect to a reference point changes with time, the object is said to be in motion; if it does not change, the object is at rest.
  • Displacement is the net change in the position of the object between two given instants of time. Its magnitude ≤ total distance travelled.
  • Average speed = total distance travelled ÷ time interval.
  • Average velocity = change in position (displacement) ÷ time interval. It needs both magnitude and direction.
  • Average acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time interval. It is in the direction of velocity when the speed increases and opposite to it when the speed decreases.
  • For motion with constant acceleration: v = u + at, s = ut + ½at² and v² = u² + 2as.
  • On a position–time graph the slope gives velocity; on a velocity–time graph the slope gives acceleration and the area under the line gives displacement.
  • Motion along a circular path with constant speed is uniform circular motion. Its average speed = 2πR ÷ T, and the velocity at any point is along the tangent.
  • Uniform circular motion is accelerated motion, because the direction of the velocity changes continuously even though the speed is constant.

First kinematic equationv = u + atUse when time is involved and displacement is not asked.
Second kinematic equations = ut + ½at²Displacement in time t with constant acceleration a.
Third kinematic equationv² = u² + 2asUse when time is neither given nor asked.
Displacement from a velocity–time graphs = area under the graph = ½(u + v)tFor a line rising from u to v in time t, the area is a trapezium.
Braking (stopping) distances = u² ÷ 2a   ·   total stopping distance = u × t(reaction) + u² ÷ 2aThe braking part grows with the square of the speed.

Note: Conversion: 1 km h⁻¹ = 1000 m ÷ 3600 s = 5/18 m s⁻¹. So 36 km h⁻¹ = 10 m s⁻¹, 54 km h⁻¹ = 15 m s⁻¹, 72 km h⁻¹ = 20 m s⁻¹ and 108 km h⁻¹ = 30 m s⁻¹.

India's Scientific Contributions

The idea that an object's speed is the distance travelled divided by the time taken was well established in ancient India.

  • Aryabhatiya (5th century CE) by Aryabhata uses this relation for the motion of celestial bodies.
  • Ganitakaumudi (14th century CE) by Narayana Pandita contains the postmen problem of Example 4.1: two postmen 210 yojanas apart, walking towards each other at 9 and 5 yojanas per day, meet after 15 days.

A yojana was a unit of distance used in ancient India.

Glossary of terms

Every term used in the chapter, in one sentence each

TermMeaning
Reference pointA fixed point, chosen by us, with respect to which the position of an object is described
PositionThe distance of the object from the reference point together with the direction in which it lies
MotionThe condition in which the position of an object changes with respect to a reference point as time passes
RestThe condition in which the position of an object does not change with respect to a reference point with time
Instant of timeA single reading of a clock, for example t = 4 s
Time intervalThe duration between two instants of time, for example from 4 s to 10 s (an interval of 6 s)
ScalarA physical quantity specified completely by its magnitude alone, such as distance, speed or time
VectorA physical quantity that needs both magnitude and direction, such as displacement, velocity or acceleration
MagnitudeThe numerical value of a physical quantity along with its unit
Distance travelledThe total length of the path covered by the object; only a magnitude, always positive
DisplacementThe net change in position between two instants; has both magnitude and direction
Uniform motionMotion in a straight line in which equal distances are covered in equal intervals of time
Non-uniform motionMotion in which unequal distances are covered in equal intervals of time
Average speedTotal distance travelled divided by the time interval
Average velocityDisplacement divided by the time interval; the average rate of change of position
Rate of changeThe ratio of a change in one quantity to the corresponding change in time
Average accelerationChange in velocity divided by the time interval; the rate of change of velocity
RetardationAcceleration acting opposite to the direction of velocity, so the body slows down
Acceleration due to gravityThe constant acceleration g = 9.8 m s⁻² with which a body falls towards the Earth
Uniform circular motionMotion along a circular path with constant speed
Time periodThe time taken to complete one revolution of a circular path
TangentA straight line that meets the circle at one and only one point; velocity in circular motion is along the tangent

The Journey Beyond

1. Derive the two remaining kinematic equations. Using v = u + at and s = ut + ½at², derive (i) s = vt − ½at² and (ii) s = ½(u + v)t. For the second equation, use the formula for the area of a trapezium from mathematics.

2. The spinning disc. Take a cardboard disc of radius about 8 cm. Write numbers 1 to 12 on the outer part (7 cm from the centre) and the letters A B C D E F on the inner part (4 cm from the centre), in the same font size. Spin the disc slowly, then faster. Why do the numbers fade or disappear while the letters remain visible? Are the speeds of the numbers and the letters the same or different? Justify your answer. (The outer marks have a larger speed, v = 2πR ÷ T, so they blur at a lower rate of spinning.)

3. The accelerometer in a smartphone. Install an app such as Phyphox and open the ‘Accelerometer (without g)’ screen. Note the readings with the phone on an outstretched palm and with the phone kept on the floor. What differences do you observe? What does this tell you about motion and acceleration in real situations?

4. Graphs with different scales. Plot the data of Table 4.4 on different graph papers using different scales, compare the graphs, and decide which scale is better and why. Repeat with a graph-plotting app.

5. Road safety project. Talk to a motor mechanic about how a vehicle's braking or stopping distance is affected by (i) wet roads, (ii) worn-out tyres, (iii) higher vehicle mass, (iv) driving at night, (v) fog, (vi) severe weather and (vii) driver reaction time. Using this information, design safety posters for your school and prepare a short skit to present in the assembly.
